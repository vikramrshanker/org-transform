The evidence in two minutes

Better tools are changing
how work is divided.

AI has improved individual tasks. There is also evidence of a deeper change: organizations are separating information collection from judgment, moving routine work into systems, and changing the roles of reviewers and specialists. The economic return depends on how that new division of work performs.

01

Capability is the starting point

Benchmarks show a wider technical range; workplace studies show gains in bounded tasks. Neither measures an entire organization’s transformation. [1],[10],[11]

02

Workflows are changing

DBS has deployed agent-assisted credit preparation. IBM changed how employees enter HR services. These are changes to the process and its responsibilities, beyond adding a writing tool. [22],[23]

03

Human work moves

A recruiting experiment automated information collection while preserving human hiring decisions. A software study found production gains accompanied by a shift toward automated review. [24],[25]

04

Redesign is not a proven formula

Some interventions improve outcomes; others move the bottleneck or create transition costs. The evidence supports testing specific designs, not assuming that fewer people or more automation means a better business.

How to read this report. Sections 1–5 review evidence, including documented workflow changes. Sections 6–10 examine organizational consequences, implementation, economics and opportunities. Interpretations and illustrative designs are labeled. “AI” includes several technologies in business surveys; “generative AI” is narrower. Observation dates are shown because a study published in 2026 may describe much older tools. This is a selected evidence review, not a meta-analysis or a current model ranking.
01 / Observed capability

AI can handle longer tasks.
Reliability changes the answer.

METR’s TH1.1 benchmark shows progress from minutes to hours of human-equivalent task difficulty. Requiring 80% success sharply reduces that horizon. [1]

The tasks are mainly software, machine learning and cybersecurity with clear goals. This does not establish full-job capability. [1]

It uses different tasks from OSWorld 1.0; lower scores do not show that models regressed.

Price has fallen for a fixed level of performance

Epoch’s March 2025 analysis found steep historical declines in the price of reaching fixed benchmark scores. Matching GPT-4’s science-question performance became roughly 40 times cheaper per year over the measured period. Rates varied widely by benchmark. This is evidence of past price improvement, not a forecast and not the cost of operating a business process. [4]

What is established: the technical range has widened. The probability of finishing a specific workflow at an acceptable cost still has to be measured in that workflow.

02 / Observed adoption

There is no single adoption rate.

A business counts as an adopter if one qualifying use exists. A worker counts as an adopter when that person uses the tool. Neither measure tells us how much of the work has changed. The denominator is part of the finding.

A break in the US series: on November 17, 2025, Census broadened the question from AI used to produce goods or services to AI used in any business function. Joining the old and new rates into one growth curve would overstate what the data show. The May figure above is a dated snapshot, not a claim about September prevalence. [5]

Size and intensity matter

In the Census May analysis, 37% of firms with at least 250 employees used AI, versus less than 20% of firms with fewer than five. A separate Census supplement found 18% adoption when counting firms and 32% when weighting firms by employment. The latter is the share of employment at adopting firms, not the share of workers personally using AI. [5],[6]

Europe shows a similar size gradient: 20% of covered enterprises used AI in 2025, rising to 55% among large enterprises. Eurostat excludes businesses with fewer than ten workers, so its headline cannot be directly compared with the US figure. [8]

Use within firms is often limited. In the Census supplement, 57% of adopters used AI in three or fewer business functions; 66% used it only to augment work. Separately, an August 2026 worker study found adoption spread across many tasks but below half of workers within most of them. Technical exposure is not the same as actual use. [6],[9]

What is established: AI reaches many kinds of work, with sharp differences in depth and frequency. The number of businesses that have not adopted is not, by itself, a market size.

03 / Measured outcomes

Task gains are real.
They do not add up automatically.

The strongest studies compare similar work with and without access to AI. Their results should be read in their original units. Faster completion, more completed tasks, better work and higher profit are different outcomes.

The negative coding result has an important follow-up

METR’s February 2026 update studied newer tools and more developers. The original participants’ estimated time change was −18% (95% interval −38% to +9%); new participants’ was −4% (−15% to +9%). Both intervals include no effect. Selection changed because developers increasingly declined tasks without AI, and parallel agent use complicated time measurement. The authors believe gains are now more likely, but do not present these estimates as a reliable measure of their size. [15]

A separate METR survey of 349 technical workers found median perceived gains of 1.4–2 times in value and three times in speed. This is useful evidence of users’ experience. It is a selected sample reporting imagined alternatives, not an experimental measurement of output. [16]

At the company level, evidence is mixed

A four-country study of nearly 6,000 executives reported that over 80% of firms saw no effect on productivity or employment over the preceding three years. A different CFO survey found reported 2025 output-per-worker gains averaging 1.8%; gains implied by respondents’ revenue and headcount answers were smaller. These studies use different samples and questions. Neither licenses a claim that AI has no value, or that all firms have captured it. [18],[19]

Danish research linked adoption surveys to administrative records and found no detectable average earnings or recorded-hours effects two years after ChatGPT’s launch, ruling out effects above 2% in that setting. Tasks and occupational moves did change. This constrains claims about immediate labor-market transformation; it does not settle longer-term effects. [17]

What is established: AI can improve particular work. How much becomes additional output, lower expense, better quality or personal time depends on what happens after the task is finished.

04 / Documented workflow changes

The unit of change is becoming
the whole process.

A workflow connects a request to an outcome through people, information, decisions and actions. Improving one task leaves those connections intact. Redesign changes them: who gathers information, where work waits, what requires approval, and who handles the difficult cases.

The cases below show several kinds of change. They provide stronger evidence that a process has changed than that an entire organization has become more productive. Experiments, company reports and targets are labeled separately.

Health care · Clinical documentation · Institutional study account

Permanente: the conversation becomes the draft record

With patient consent, an ambient scribe drafts notes from the visit; physicians edit them in a workflow integrated with the medical record. Clinical decisions remain with the physician. [31]

The institution estimates 1,794 working days of documentation time saved. Among surveyed patients, 47% reported less physician attention to the computer; 39% reported more direct conversation.

Change in work: documenting from memory becomes editing a prepared record. Use remained concentrated despite broad availability. Observational results support reduced burden and better reported interaction; they do not establish more appointments, fewer clinicians or a financial return.

Changes to expertise and coordination are also being tested

In the P&G experiment, AI-assisted proposals combined commercial and technical perspectives more evenly; individuals with AI matched teams without it on the bounded task. That is evidence that some expertise can cross functional boundaries more cheaply. It is not evidence that P&G eliminated departments or permanently replaced teams. [13]

The office-work experiment provides a useful contrast: individual AI access saved email time without a detectable change in the amount or composition of work. A tool can improve a person’s day while leaving coordination among people largely intact. [12]

Does redesign cause better returns?

McKinsey’s 2026 survey links workflow redesign with reported financial success: roughly three-quarters of its high performers reported redesign, versus one-quarter of others. High performers were defined partly by reported EBIT impact. This is an association: stronger firms may both redesign and perform better, and successful respondents may describe their changes more favorably. [20]

DORA’s 2025 research similarly associated more AI use with both higher software throughput and greater instability. It points to the surrounding delivery system as an important condition, but does not randomly test alternative organization designs. [28]

Evidence boundary: workflow change is observable, and some interventions have improved downstream outcomes. The sources reviewed do not establish a general causal return to “organizational transformation,” or prove that every firm needs a wholesale redesign.

05 / Reported constraints

Non-adoption has several causes.

The OECD asked 5,232 SMEs across seven countries about generative AI in late 2024. Among non-adopters, lack of fit with their work was the most common barrier. Concerns about legal issues, information handling and skills were also frequent. These are owners’ and managers’ assessments, not independently tested diagnoses. [21]

Organizational friction and technical limits coexist. A process change must work within both.

What is established: there is no single “adoption gap” to sell into. Some firms lack skills or a workable deployment. Others may have little useful work for the current tools, or too little volume to justify the cost.

From evidence to interpretation

The following is our analysis. These are implications to test, not findings established by the studies above.

06 / Organizational consequences · Analysis

The deeper change is who knows,
who acts and who decides.

The following interpretation draws on the cases above. AI can change the cost of obtaining expertise, coordinating work and checking results. If those costs change enough, a business can change how it divides work. That is a different proposition from giving everyone an assistant.

Six organizational choices that the technology reopens
DimensionPossible changeWhat still has to be solved
Roles and specializationOne person can perform a broader span of work with AI assistance. Specialists may spend less time preparing standard outputs and more on review and unusual cases.Access to a specialist-like answer does not confer the ability to judge it. Measure quality on difficult cases and preserve a path for developing expertise.
Handoffs and teamsA shared system can gather inputs in parallel and keep a case moving across functions. Some status meetings, copying and routine transfers may become unnecessary.Someone must own the whole outcome. Otherwise faster upstream work simply increases the queue elsewhere.
Authority and managementRoutine actions can operate within explicit limits; managers may focus on exceptions, policies and performance. Standard rules can support more local execution.Decide separately who may prepare, recommend, approve and execute. Cheaper information could flatten some structures while centralizing control over others; the cases do not establish a universal direction.
Knowledge and memoryRules held by experienced employees can become reusable instructions, reference material and examples. Each resolved exception can improve the process.Know-how must be elicited, reconciled and maintained. A document archive does not tell an agent which rule applies, which source is current or when an exception is justified.
Staffing and incentivesCapacity may shift toward customer work, quality or growth. Hiring needs may change even without immediate layoffs. New work appears in testing, exception handling and system maintenance.Specify what happens to saved capacity. Employees asked to reveal their expertise also need a reason to participate; an output quota can reward volume while hiding quality loss.
Boundaries of the firmWork previously bought from a specialist or outsourced provider may move inside; a managed service may instead absorb a workflow the customer cannot run.Compare the full cost of ownership. Internal automation introduces maintenance and accountability, while outsourcing can create dependence on the provider.

Two different kinds of advantage

An existing firm has customers, operating history and experienced staff. Its opportunity may be to turn that knowledge into a process more people can run. Its constraint is changing a working business without interrupting service or losing the people who understand it.

A new firm can design roles and systems together. It may need fewer handoffs because it does not inherit the old departmental boundaries. But it lacks the incumbent’s relationships and hard-won exceptions. Lower coordination cost is a hypothesis to demonstrate, not an automatic advantage of being new.

For an acquisition strategy, the distinction matters. A business that looks inefficient may contain valuable judgment disguised as manual work. Removing that work before understanding it can destroy the asset one intended to improve.

07 / Implementation · Evidence and analysis

The hard part is making
a different process work every day.

What sat behind the DBS deployments

DBS reports a sequence of investments in process, data and organization. These measures describe different programs; they should not be added together as “AI savings.”

Our interpretation: a production workflow is a maintained system of rules, information and responsibilities. The implementation work is therefore broader than connecting a model to company files. The following is a practical way to examine it, not a claim that every successful firm followed the same sequence.

A concrete example: the quote-to-job process

Consider a service business receiving inquiries, gathering job details, preparing quotes, approving prices and scheduling work. The diagram is illustrative, not a documented deployment. It shows the difference between accelerating a step and changing how a case moves through the business.

The changed design requires current price rules, reliable availability, permission to act and a workable exception route. If every quote is unusual, it may create more review work than it removes. If the business cannot deliver more jobs, faster quoting may improve service without increasing revenue.

The experiment that would distinguish these designs: compare the current process, task assistance and the redesigned workflow on similar cases. Measure time to an accepted outcome, total human effort, rework, customer conversion and full operating cost. Include difficult cases and a period after launch support declines. This would test the incremental value of redesign instead of assuming it.

08 / Economic mechanism

A saved minute is not yet a dollar.

A tool can make one step much faster while leaving the business almost unchanged. If a quote takes ten minutes to draft but three days to approve, cheaper drafting does not remove the delay. If sales are fixed, additional production capacity may sit idle. If errors are expensive, review can consume the saving.

Conceptual diagram. Each transition needs evidence. It is not an estimated conversion model.

The useful unit is the cost of an accepted result

That cost includes model usage, human checking, failed attempts, systems work and ongoing maintenance. It should be compared with the current process at the same quality level. A reduction in token prices changes only one part of this calculation.

A 90% compute-price cut reduces total AI-process cost by only 4.3% here. Actual quality, volume, failures and staffing decisions could reverse the conclusion.

For an owner, the immediate gain might be a shorter day. For a growing firm, it might be serving more customers without proportional hiring. For a fixed-volume operation, it might become a cost saving only after staffing or contractor commitments change. A business case should specify which result it expects.

09 / Possible consequences

Who might gain—and where an opening might remain.

The evidence favors examining specific workflows before making a broad claim about “transforming a company.” It does not yet identify a winning sector, buyer or company concept. The following are causal hypotheses.

What this changes in the brainstorms

The existing synthesis proposes workflow discovery, diligence, operating intelligence and implementation. The research makes the case for measuring a process more concrete. It does not establish willingness to pay for a diagnostic product, repeat demand after the first assessment or defensibility against existing software and service providers.

Four possible needs follow from this analysis. They differ in buyer, delivery burden and potential for repeat revenue. None is validated by the deployment cases alone.

Where the organizational work might create a business

There is also a different route: capture the gains by operating or acquiring the underlying business. That requires operating competence and attractive purchase economics, not just evidence that a process can be automated. For a small firm, the commercially useful package may combine software and hands-on operation; enterprise case studies do not establish its price or market size.

Low adoption alone is weak evidence for a business. The more useful opening would be a repeated, valuable workflow where present tools underperform for a specific, removable reason—and where the customer will pay to remove it.

10 / What remains unknown

The next evidence should distinguish demand from possibility.

Questions that would change our view
QuestionEvidence worth collectingResult that would weaken the opportunity
Is the work valuable enough?Actual volume, time, delay, rework and spending for one recurring process.Too little volume; no backlog, cash cost or customer problem to remove.
Can current tools do it reliably?Comparable real cases across the old process, task assistance and workflow redesign; blind quality review; total human minutes and costs.Gains disappear after review or difficult cases are included.
What prevents adoption?Observe the workflow and trial a narrow intervention. Separate data access, capability, skills, ownership and incentives.Cheap existing tools solve it, or the apparent barrier is rational lack of demand.
Does capacity become value?Before/after throughput, customer outcomes and expense, ideally with a comparable control group.Local speed improves but the bottleneck, revenue and costs do not move.
Is there a repeatable business?Several independent customers with the same need, paid use, repeat deployment effort and ongoing support cost.Each customer requires a different solution; custom labor consumes the margin.

Timing remains open. Better models can remove a technical barrier, while coordination and customer behavior may move more slowly. Re-test technical limits after meaningful model changes. Re-test the economics when price, volume or review requirements change. Neither an exponential benchmark curve nor a slow adoption survey supplies a dependable company-building timetable.

    Official enterprise statistics. Survey covers enterprises with at least 10 workers in covered sectors. Excludes microbusinesses; not directly comparable with US BTOS.

  9. Bick et al.: What Work Does Generative AI Do? ↗
    August 2026; abstract revised September 7

    Working paper; author abstract. Nationally representative worker survey linked to tasks. Self-reported use; exposure and platform chat categories do not measure the same thing.

  10. Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond: Generative AI at Work ↗
    QJE, May 2025; earlier deployment observations

    Peer-reviewed field study; staggered rollout. 5,172 support agents at one firm. Updated published estimate is 15%; do not mix with earlier 14% working-paper version.

  11. Cui et al.: AI and software developer productivity ↗
    August 20, 2025 version

    Three randomized company field experiments. 4,867 developers. Pooled effect of adoption estimated using randomized access; 26.08%, standard error 10.3 percentage points. Company-level estimates are noisy; Microsoft-affiliated coauthors.

  12. Dillon et al.: Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI ↗
    AER: Insights, forthcoming; abstract accessed September 20, 2026

    Randomized field experiment; accepted article abstract. 66 firms, 7,137 workers, six months. Two-hour estimate concerns users in the second half, not all assigned workers. Microsoft-affiliated coauthors.

  13. Dell’Acqua et al.: The Cybernetic Teammate ↗
    April 2025 working-paper version

    Randomized innovation exercise; author abstract and HBS researcher account. 776 P&G professionals in this version. Short product-development exercise; does not measure enduring team replacement. P&G supported the research. Researcher account ↗

  14. METR: early-2025 developer experiment ↗
    July 10, 2025; early-2025 tools

    Randomized task-level experiment. 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 tasks in familiar repositories. Time increase 19%, 95% interval 2%–39%. Narrow and dated setting.

  15. METR: developer-productivity follow-up ↗
    February 24, 2026; experiment begun August 2025

    Experimental follow-up with selection problems. 57 developers, 800+ tasks. Participation and task selection changed; both reported subgroup intervals cross zero. Not a clean estimate of current average gains.

  16. METR: AI usage survey ↗
    May 11, 2026; February–April observations

    Self-report survey of technical workers. 349 respondents; selected sample. Perceived counterfactual speed and value are not experimentally measured output.

  17. Humlum & Vestergaard: Still Waters, Rapid Currents ↗
    March 13, 2026 version; two years after ChatGPT launch

    Working paper; surveys linked to administrative records. Danish difference-in-differences estimates. Null average earnings/hours effects do not rule out task change, later effects or different outcomes elsewhere.

  18. Yotzov et al.: Firm Data on AI ↗
    March 24, 2026

    Working paper; stratified executive surveys. Nearly 6,000 executives across US, UK, Germany and Australia. Retrospective impact is self-reported. Future expectations are not observed results.

  19. Atlanta Fed, Richmond Fed & Duke: CFO evidence ↗
    March 25, 2026; November 2025–January 2026 fieldwork

    Primary survey analysis; 748 responses. Reported output-per-worker gains exceed gains implied by reported revenue and headcount. Neither measure is an audited causal estimate.

  20. McKinsey: The State of AI in 2026 ↗
    August 25, 2026; May 4–June 8 fieldwork

    Consultancy survey; 1,719 respondents in 97 nations. 36% work at firms with over $1bn revenue; country-GDP weighting. Self-reported use and impact, not representative of all firms by count. Publisher sells AI consulting.

  21. OECD: Generative AI and the SME Workforce ↗
    November 5, 2025; October–December 2024 fieldwork

    Representative telephone survey; 5,232 SMEs in seven countries. Includes one-person businesses through 249 employees. Barrier percentages refer to non-adopters; multiple answers allowed. Older adoption snapshot, not September 2026 prevalence.

  22. DBS: agentic credit-assessment rollout ↗
    August 19, 2026

    Company deployment announcement. 150-person pilot followed by rollout to about 1,500 staff. The 30% time reduction is a goal, not a measured result. No controlled outcomes disclosed.

  23. IBM CHRO: redesigning HR service delivery ↗
    2025 account; describes 2017–2024 changes

    First-person management account. Channel closures, policy simplification and automation changed together. Reported containment and budget changes do not isolate generative AI. IBM also sells these systems.

  24. Jabarian & Henkel: Voice AI in Firms ↗
    September 11, 2026 revision; March–June 2025 experiment

    Randomized field experiment; full methods reviewed. Philippine entry-level service recruitment; human hiring authority retained. Google Cloud support. One author later accepted an unpaid role at the partner firm.

  25. He et al.: AI Writes Faster Than Humans Can Review ↗
    July 2, 2026; January 2024–April 2026 observations

    Longitudinal single-company study; preprint. Nonrandom adoption. The 2.09× statistic counts authored pull requests per active developer, distinct from the mandate’s merged-output target. Quality proxies miss latent defects.

  26. DBS: platforms, processes and people ↗
    June 11, 2025

    Company account of deployment infrastructure. Reported delivery-time reduction accompanies shared data and model infrastructure. No counterfactual or cost breakdown; large-bank infrastructure may not transfer economically to an SME.

  27. Klarna: 2025 Form 20-F ↗
    2026 filing; year ended December 31, 2025

    Company regulatory filing; narrative disclosures, PDF page 188. 80% chat share and approximately $59m savings are company-reported measures. A filing is not independent causal validation of AI savings; customers retained human support.

  28. Google Cloud: 2025 DORA findings ↗
    September 23, 2025

    Primary research summary; survey of nearly 5,000 technology professionals. Reported associations, not random assignment. More AI use correlated with both throughput and instability. Google Cloud sells AI and development products.

  29. DBS: customer assistants and agentic rollout ↗
    July 28, 2026; first-half 2026 outcomes

    Company deployment and operating-metrics announcement. Joy outcomes precede its July agentic upgrade. Eligible users are not active users. Satisfaction baseline and causal contribution are undisclosed.

  30. DBS: 2025 CEO reflections ↗
    2025 annual report; published 2026

    Management account of bank-wide transformation. Economic value covers data analytics and all AI/ML, not just generative AI. Program counts and management estimates are not controlled returns.

  31. Permanente: ambient AI scribe deployment ↗
    April 7, 2025; 63-week evaluation

    Deploying institution’s account of its NEJM Catalyst study. Observational adoption and patient surveys. Primary institutional summary reviewed; journal full text was inaccessible. No demonstrated increase in patient capacity or profit. Rollout and integration account ↗

  32. DBS: 2024 CIO statement ↗
    2024 annual report; published 2025

    Management account of process and control changes. Manual hours are cumulative; incident reductions have different definitions. Digitalization and conventional ML are included. These are not isolated generative-AI effects.

Scope. This report tests claims relevant to AI capability and adoption. It does not validate the other claims in the supplied research memo, size a market, choose a sector or recommend a company.
