Private equity, returns and capital

Operating performance and liquidity are becoming harder to separate—and more important to distinguish.

00 / Executive brief

Better exits have not removed the pressure to perform.

Private equity’s challenge is no longer well described as simply waiting for the exit market to reopen. A transaction can create liquidity without improving a business; a business can improve while its owners still struggle to sell at the desired price.

01

Recovery is uneven

Bain reports 2025 buyout-backed exit value of $717bn, up 47%. It also finds distributions below 15% of NAV for four consecutive years and exit holding periods around seven years.[1], [11]

02

Time changes the return

The same 2.0× equity proceeds imply 14.9% annualized over five years and 10.4% over seven, with all cash paid at exit. Delays matter even without a write-down.

03

Liquidity has more routes

Jefferies estimates $240bn of secondaries in 2025: $125bn LP-led and $115bn GP-led. These transactions change who holds exposure; they do not necessarily end the underlying investment.[3]

04

Operating proof becomes valuable

A credible improvement must survive implementation costs, working-capital needs and buyer diligence. A generated investment memo cannot substitute for that evidence.

Scope. This is a market and operating-model analysis, not an allocation recommendation. Reported market observations precede our explicit financial illustrations. Global buyout data should not be treated as the economics of small owner-operated acquisitions.
01 / Market evidence

A valuation, a sale and a distribution are different events.

Bain estimates an inventory of 32,000 unsold buyout-backed companies worth $3.8tn. That is evidence of substantial remaining exposure, not a count of distressed sellers. Its “12 is the new 5” comparison models a shift from about 5% annual EBITDA growth to roughly 10–12% to reach a 2.5× five-year return under changed entry, leverage and exit assumptions. It is a scenario, not a universal required growth rate.[1], [2]

The financing environment increases the value of better operating information, but does not guarantee demand for another software platform. A sponsor’s urgency can produce more spending on implementation, tighter budgets for experiments, or both. The buying decision will depend on how directly the work changes an investment outcome.

02 / Documented cases

Follow the cash, then examine the business.

Completed strategic sale / C.H.I. / 2022

A realized sale with an operating story—and a selection problem

$3.0bnNucor’s completed purchase consideration
21% → 35%EBITDA margin reported by KKR
10×money multiple reported by KKR

What happened

Nucor completed its acquisition of C.H.I. Overhead Doors in June 2022. KKR described an equity program covering all 800 employees, improved safety and product quality, revenue more than doubling and EBITDA more than tripling. It reported margins rising from 21% to 35%.[7], [8]

What it suggests

The useful operating questions are specific: which quality failures were removed, how the workforce participated and how much capital was required? These mechanisms are more transferable than a headline sponsor return. This case predates current generative AI deployment.

Evidence boundary. The sale is confirmed by the acquirer; operational attribution and the return multiple come from the sponsor. A selected success does not establish average PE performance or isolate the effect of any one intervention.

IPO / Medline / December 2025

A large offering split between deleveraging and owner liquidity

$7.048bnnet IPO proceeds before other offering expenses
$4.023bnspecified term-loan repayments
$1.970bnpurchase or redemption from pre-IPO owners

What happened

Medline’s Q1 2026 filing reconciles the completed IPO, including the underwriters’ option. Of the proceeds, $5.078bn funded newly issued partnership units; $731m repaid euro term loans and $3.292bn repaid US dollar term loans. Another $1.051bn plus $919m funded purchases or redemptions from pre-IPO owners.[9]

What it suggests

An IPO can simultaneously create a public price, lower company leverage and supply some owner liquidity. Treating the entire offering as sponsor cash realization would misstate the event. The remaining ownership still carries operating and market risk.

Evidence boundary. Rounded amounts may not sum perfectly. The filing does not allocate every owner redemption to particular PE LPs, and debt repayment is not a fund distribution.

Market mechanism / LP secondaries / 2025

Selling a fund interest can mean accepting a discount

92%average buyout portfolio price / NAV
78%venture and growth price / NAV
70%real-estate price / NAV

What happened

Jefferies reports differing 2025 LP portfolio prices by strategy. Pricing also varied with fund age and transaction structure. These are prices for portfolios sold in the secondary market, not discounts applicable to every asset.[3]

What it suggests

Liquidity can have an explicit cost. A seller exchanges uncertain future distributions for cash today; the buyer takes the remaining duration, valuation and funding risks. Better portfolio data may reduce uncertainty, but cannot eliminate economic disagreement.

Evidence boundary. Adviser estimates and selected transactions. Reported NAV can lag current conditions; a discount to NAV is not automatically a discount to fair value.

03 / Capital structures

Different tools solve different timing problems.

Where cash comes from, and what remains at risk
StructureSource of cash / purposeTradeoff to examine
Trade saleA buyer purchases the asset; fund receives net equity proceeds.Closing conditions, debt payoff, escrow and transaction costs determine actual realizable cash.
IPO and later sell-downsNew investors fund primary shares or purchase existing exposure.Listing is not a complete exit; lockups, remaining holdings and market prices still matter.
Continuation vehicleNew or rolling investors finance the asset in a new structure.Pricing, fees, carry, conflicts and the choice to sell or roll must be assessed.
NAV financingA lender advances against portfolio value or distributions.Interest, collateral, covenants and possible recall obligations; cash arrives with a corresponding liability.
Dividend recapitalizationA portfolio company borrows to distribute cash to owners.Company leverage rises; future operating cash must service the debt.
Long-duration holding companyCapital stays invested under a different holding model.Reduces pressure for a scheduled exit but does not remove return discipline, financing needs or agency problems.

ILPA’s NAV guidance recommends LP advisory committee consent for distribution-funded facilities regardless of the partnership agreement’s language. Its concern includes interest cost, recallable distributions and effects on reported IRR and DPI. These are investor-governance recommendations, not a claim that all such facilities destroy value.[4]

Continuation transactions likewise need a fair process. ILPA issued guidance in 2023 and published a new draft in June 2026 focused on conflicts, elections, pricing and transparency. The checked page describes finalization as still forthcoming; the draft should not be presented as settled rules.[5], [6]

A provider evaluating an investment must therefore maintain two reconciliations: the operating company’s cash and the fund’s cash. Combining them in one “value created” number obscures who financed the distribution and who retains the risk.

04 / Return mechanics

Small operating changes can matter. So can small assumption changes.

The following model deliberately holds most variables constant. Entry EBITDA is $10m, enterprise value is 10× EBITDA, debt is $50m and initial equity is $50m. Exit is after five years, with $30m remaining debt. All scenarios assume the same debt paydown. Fees, taxes, interim distributions and management dilution are excluded. This is sensitivity analysis, not an underwritten transaction.

Illustrative five-year equity outcomes
ScenarioExit EBITDA / multipleEquity proceedsMOIC / annualized return
No earnings growth$10.00m / 10×$70.00m1.40× / 7.0%
5% annual EBITDA growth$12.76m / 10×$97.63m1.95× / 14.3%
10% annual EBITDA growth$16.11m / 10×$131.05m2.62× / 21.3%
5% growth, multiple falls$12.76m / 8×$72.10m1.44× / 7.6%

Equity proceeds equal exit EBITDA × exit multiple − remaining debt. MOIC equals proceeds / initial equity. With only one exit cash flow, the annualized return is MOIC raised to 1/5, minus one. A real model must derive debt paydown from cash generation rather than assume it; interest, capital spending and working capital can change the result materially.

Now connect a process improvement to this model. An assumed $500,000 of durable incremental annual EBITDA is worth $5m at an unchanged 10× exit multiple, before implementation cost and other adjustments. But hours saved are not EBITDA until they reduce expense or generate profitable additional output. A buyer may also reject the adjustment if it lacks a track record. Counting time saved, cost savings and valuation uplift as three separate gains would double-count the same mechanism.

05 / Operating evidence

Turn a diligence claim into a maintained operating record.

What would make an AI-enabled operating gain credible
ClaimOperational proofFinancial reconciliation
Fewer support staff neededStable resolution quality, escalation and demand; actual staffing or outsourcing change.Net payroll or vendor savings after software, review and transition expense.
Higher sales capacityComparable leads, conversion, retention and delivery capability.Incremental gross profit, net of acquisition cost and fulfillment expense.
Better purchasingLike-for-like specification, quality and delivery reliability.Realized purchase-price variance after volume, mix, freight and rebates.
Faster collectionsInvoice-to-cash time and dispute rates on comparable accounts.Cash release separated from EBITDA; verify it is sustainable, not a one-time timing shift.

Historical academic research finds PE effects vary substantially by transaction type, sponsor and economic conditions. That heterogeneity argues against applying a single transformation percentage to every portfolio company.[10]

The organizational work is concrete: someone must own the baseline, authorize the operational change and accept the financial measurement. An investment team can sponsor the project, but the portfolio company supplies most of the data and execution. A product sold to the sponsor that adds work without helping the operator can fail at that boundary.

06 / Opportunity hypotheses

The opening is repeatable execution with credible attribution.

Potential winners, pressure points and company tests
PositionWhy the trend mattersOpportunity test
Sector-specialist operatorsThey can recognize recurring operational constraints and interpret exceptions.Can one intervention improve several companies after including local implementation cost?
Sponsors with weak operating visibilityMore reporting may still leave uncertainty about actual cash drivers.Would a maintained operational record change an investment or exit decision enough to pay for it?
Lenders and secondary buyersLonger duration and complex structures increase the importance of reliable underlying data.Does better information improve underwriting or pricing, beyond the existing reporting stack?
Generic diligence summarizersDrafting becomes easier while evidence verification remains demanding.Can the provider verify a consequential claim or own an ongoing workflow, rather than merely summarize files?
Acquisition or holding-company buildersThey can capture operating gains directly.Do entry price, operator skill and financing support the strategy without assumed multiple expansion?

The brainstorm idea of moving from diligence into portfolio operating intelligence is coherent, but the bridge is unproven. Diligence is episodic and buyer-led; operations are continuous and management-led. Recurring revenue requires a recurring decision with an owner and a budget. A useful wedge might be one cash-critical workflow in one sector, with diligence creating the baseline and post-close work keeping it current.

07 / What would change the view

Prove that better information changes cash outcomes.

Open questions that distinguish a business from a useful tool
QuestionNext evidenceThesis weakens if…
Is operating proof the missing input?Review recent investment and exit decisions with deal teams and operators.Price, strategic fit or market timing dominates; more process data changes little.
Who controls the recurring budget?Paid pilots with named sponsor and company owners; explicit post-close use.The customer disappears after diligence or ownership transfer.
Can gains be attributed?Comparable baselines, implementation logs and finance-approved benefit reconciliation.Reported savings are capacity estimates or disappear after transition costs.
Can the product survive existing systems?Compare against ERP reporting, portfolio-monitoring tools and operating consultants.The need is already met or requires bespoke advisory work each time.
Does evidence survive an exit?Test whether independent buyer diligence accepts the claimed recurring gain.Adjustments are rejected or only accelerate cash temporarily.

Revisit this report as realized distributions, holding periods and transaction structures change. A reopened exit market may reduce urgency for some services. It would not remove the need to distinguish a better business from a more convenient financing arrangement.
