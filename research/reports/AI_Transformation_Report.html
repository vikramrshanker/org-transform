AI and the Transformation of Ordinary Companies: Succession, Private Equity, Agents and Compute

September 20, 2026 · Vikram Shanker · Startup Ideation

The memo in one page

Ordinary companies are about to be changed by AI from four directions at once: who owns them, who runs the change inside them, how customers find them, and what technology they own. In each case the scarce thing is the ability to change how work gets done inside a company, not capital or software.

Recurring constraintWhere it shows upDoes AI remove it?

The story in five sentences. About half of US employer businesses are owned by someone 55 or older, but the predicted wave of sales has not arrived: reported small-business sales have been flat near 9,600 a year since 2018. Private equity needs those companies and needs 10 to 12% annual profit growth to make deals work, yet most firms cannot execute operational change inside a company with no technology leader. AI is good at the clerical and coordination work inside these companies, and it widens the gap between firms that can deploy it and firms that cannot. At the same time, AI assistants are becoming the way customers find and buy from businesses, and the assistant platform becomes the landlord. So the most interesting companies are operators that own the work and use AI inside it, or that carry out the change for owners who cannot, not tools sold to small fragmented buyers.

What is overstated

  • "The silver tsunami of business sales is here." It has been forecast since 2010. Owners keep working, and the estate tax no longer forces a sale below $15M per person.

  • "PE returns come from operations." Leverage and rising multiples produced 59% of buyout returns for 2010 to 2022 deals. That engine is gone, and the replacement is unproven.

  • "AI will lift portfolio company profit quickly." In 2026, 39% of sponsors expect no material financial impact this year and 6% see high impact now.

  • "Shopping is moving into the chatbot." OpenAI shut down in-chat checkout after six months with 12 to 30 live merchants. Google search revenue grew 17% in Q2 2026.

  • "Being early to connectors is like the App Store in 2009." The platform sees demand, conversion and price, so it can rank, charge and copy.

  • "Owning GPUs is cheaper" and "owning GPUs is pure signaling." Both are too strong. Vendor cost studies compare against inflated cloud prices, but rental prices rose 40% in six months and frontier token prices stopped falling.

1. Business succession and aging owners

Thesis. About half of US employer businesses are owned by someone 55 or older, but the market splits in two. Above $2M of annual profit, buyers outnumber good sellers and prices are high. Below $1M, where more than 95% of firms sit, there is almost no institutional buyer, and most of those firms will close or pass informally. The predicted wave of sales has not shown up in the data, because owners keep working and nothing forces them to sell.

How many businesses, and how big (Q1)

  • The US has about 6M employer firms and 30M businesses with no employees (SBA, Census). Only about 650,000 firms have 20 to 499 employees.

  • 51% of employer-business owners are 55 or older (Census). The advocacy figure built on this is 2.9M firms, 32.1M employees and $6.5T of revenue (Project Equity).

  • About 200,000 firms have $10M to $1B of revenue (NCMM).

  • Households hold $16.56T of equity in unincorporated businesses, and the top 1% hold 53% of it (Fed via FRED).

Plans, and why owners do not act (Q5, Q6)

Surveys report anywhere from 8% to 54% of owners with a "plan", depending on the definition. A fair reading: 40 to 50% say something is written, 10 to 15% have a plan with advisors behind it, and under 10% could start a sale tomorrow. Gallup finds 74% of employer-firm owners intend to sell or transfer (Gallup), so intent is not the gap.

The reasons separate cleanly. Identity: 78% of owners without a plan say they enjoy running the business too much. Money: the business funds their lifestyle and their retirement, so they hold for value. Information: only 15% have a professional valuation, and 62% of buyers say listings are overpriced. Tax: the 2025 budget law made the $15M per person estate exemption permanent, which removed the one deadline that forced planning. Process: only 23% of sellers will carry a note, while 62% of buyers want one.

What makes a business hard to sell, and what preparation is worth (Q7, Q8, Q14)

Buyers walk away from owner dependence, weak records (14% of sellers lack adequate books), customer concentration, profit too small to pay both the loan and a manager, and adjustments to profit they do not believe. The core problem is that owners keep books to minimize tax, then ask buyers to pay for profit that the tax return does not show.

Axial reviewed 75 broken deals in 2025. Disputes found in the earnings review caused 21.3% of breaks, up from 10.6% in 2023, while financing fell from 21.3% to 10.7% (Axial). Financial cleanup before going to market is now the most valuable single step.

Financing (Q10)

Capital is abundant above $2M of profit and thin below it. SBA lending hit a record $44.8B in fiscal 2025 (SBA), and acquisition loans charge off less often than other SBA loans (1.93% against 2.71%). But the 2025 rule changes tightened the buyer pool: 10% minimum equity, seller notes count only if frozen for the whole loan term, and from March 1, 2026 every owner must be a US citizen (policy tracker). Immigrants own roughly a fifth of US businesses. The loan cap of $5M binds at about $2M of profit, and above that buyers need seller notes, rollover equity or mezzanine debt at 9.5 to 11.5%.

What happens to firms with no successor (Q11, Q12, Q18)

Owner retirement is the second most common reason employer firms close, behind low sales (SBA). Against about 1.2M establishment closures a year there are roughly 10,000 broker-reported sales and a few thousand larger deals. Owners planning to close earn a median $20,000, so most closures are not lost assets.

The real loss is the viable firm earning $300K to $1M in a small town. Buyers of small firms live within 20 miles 60 to 68% of the time. Vermont has one broker per 23,000 firms and Florida one per 588. Employees, suppliers and the local tax base depend on these firms, and employee-owned firms show better outcomes (1.9% layoff rate against 5.1%), though healthier firms choose that path.

Sellers outnumber buyers below $1M of profit, in rural areas, in owner-dependent trades and practices, and in capital-heavy manufacturing (small manufacturing prices fell 23% in Q1 2026). Buyers outnumber sellers for good service firms earning $2M to $10M.

The aging workforce inside firms

The problem is owners more than workers. Median ages: farmers 56, appraisers 53, tax preparers 48, accountants 45, all occupations 42 (BLS). First-time CPA exam takers fell 33% from 2016 to 2021. 42% of physicians are 55+. Knowledge loss is worst where the owner's license or relationships are the asset: small CPA firms, trade contractors, farms, funeral homes, engineering firms. Industries that get harder to run because customers age while staff shrink include construction (88% of contractors report hard-to-fill craft jobs), rural healthcare, trucking and local services.

What AI changes (Q17)

Two things are real so far. First, venture-funded holding companies are buying service firms and automating them. General Catalyst committed $1.5B, and Thrive Holdings committed $1B to accounting and has bought about 50 practices (Forbes). Reported results are sponsor claims, such as 31% time saved on 7,000 tax returns. None target firms below $1M of profit. Second, AI-assisted sell-side advisors (OffDeal, $17M raised; Baton, $15.5M raised) are attacking the $1M to $10M deal range that banks find too small and brokers find too complex.

What I did not find: any product that captures an owner's unwritten knowledge (pricing rules, customer history, supplier terms, how jobs get quoted) to make the firm less dependent on the owner. That is the link between this section and the enterprise compute section, and it is open.

Where an entrant could sit

  1. A buyer for good firms earning $300K to $2M, run as a permanent holding company with a shared AI back office. This is the clearest gap. Teamshares shows both that it can be done and how much capital it takes.

  2. Sale readiness sold through CPAs and wealth advisors. Clean, buyer-grade financials plus a captured record of how the owner runs the firm. Pay the advisor who holds the relationship to start the process.

  3. The $1M to $10M sell-side process. AI lowers the cost of preparing materials and finding buyers enough to serve deals banks skip.

  4. Buyer financing for the people SBA now excludes, and for deals between $5M and $15M where the SBA cap and PE minimums leave a hole.

What is overstated. Reported small-business sales were 10,312 in 2018 and 9,586 in 2025. In 2019, 75% of brokers expected more boomer sales next year; in 2025, 80% still did. The "$10T and 12M businesses" line exceeds the count of all employer firms. "Only 20 to 30% of listings sell" traces to an old broker reference guide with no denominator.

2. Mid-market private equity

Thesis. The old return engine (cheap debt and rising multiples) is gone. A deal bought today needs 10 to 12% annual profit growth to reach the same return that 5% growth delivered in 2015. Firms know this and have doubled their operating teams, but they still cannot reliably change what happens inside a 300-person company with no technology leader. Meanwhile the fund structure itself is jammed: 32,000 unsold companies, record-low cash returned to investors, and small funds that cannot raise again. The opportunity is in operating capability and in structures that are not tied to a five-year exit.

Where returns came from, and what changed (Q1)

  • Leverage and multiple expansion produced 59% of buyout returns for deals done 2010 to 2022. Operations produced 41% (McKinsey, Feb 2026).

  • In software deals, revenue growth was 52% of value, multiple expansion 42%, and margin improvement 6%. 31 of 33 deals had planned a median 5.6 points of margin gain, and actual gains "badly trailed" (Bain).

  • Bain's "12 is the new 5": a 2015 deal used 50% debt at 6 to 7% and bought at 10x. A 2025 deal uses 30 to 40% debt at 8 to 9% and buys at 14x. The first needed 5% annual profit growth to make 2.5 times its money, and the second needs 10 to 12% (Bain 2026).

  • Margin gains arrive late: about 6 points in the final year of ownership and about 1 point a year before that (McKinsey). That is either slow execution or dressing the company for sale.

  • The median entry multiple was a record 11.8x in 2025. Among companies Lincoln tracks, 19.5% cannot cover their fixed charges from cash flow (Lincoln, Q1 2026).

Supply of companies against capital (Q2, Q3)

  • About 200,000 US firms have $10M to $1B of revenue. PE owned 13,509 US companies at mid-2026, which would take about 11 years to sell at the current pace (PitchBook via Cherry Bekaert).

  • Global unspent buyout capital is $1.3T, and 40% of it is more than two years old.

  • Price rises sharply with size: about 6x profit for $10M to $25M companies and about 10x for $100M to $250M (GF Data). The gap is widest in business services (3.6 turns) and healthcare (3.2).

  • Firms are moving down in size to find value. Deals in the $25M to $100M range jumped 56% in one quarter in 2026 (PitchBook).

  • Add-on acquisitions were 75% of US buyouts in the first half of 2026. New platforms fell 34%.

Aging owners have not made deals cheaper. Multiples are flat to up, and disagreement on price was the top reason deals failed in 2025 (Bain). Succession supplies add-on targets at 3x to 6x. It does not supply cheap platforms.

The execution gap (Q5, Q6, Q8, Q9)

The operating playbook is known: a thesis, a 100-day plan, management changes, a small set of measures, then pricing, sales, purchasing, hiring, systems, finance, acquisitions and exit preparation. The gap is in doing it.

  • 65% of PE firms replace the CEO during ownership, and more than 60% of replacements are first-time CEOs (AlixPartners 2026). 83% say an unplanned CEO change lengthens the hold.

  • Operating teams have more than doubled since 2021, but the number of portfolio companies has roughly doubled in a decade too.

  • Firms focused on one sector earned 17% against 13% for generalists from 2010 to 2022. Firms with an operating focus earn 2 to 3 points more.

  • Management fees averaged 1.6% in 2025 and co-investment demands cut fee income by about a quarter. Operating capability costs more while fee income falls. Small firms are squeezed.

Who owns AI change inside a portfolio company? In practice nobody. The typical mid-market company has no chief information officer and no data team. The job lands on the CFO or COO with help from the sponsor's operating partner, who covers many companies. An MIT study found tools bought from vendors succeeded about twice as often as internal builds (coverage). That favors outside specialists who deploy the same playbook across many companies.

Adoption is early. About 20% of portfolio companies had AI in production with measured results in Bain's survey. In 2026, 39% of sponsors expect no material financial impact this year, 6% see high impact now, and 70% expect it in 3 to 5 years. Portfolio managers are twice as satisfied with AI results as their sponsors. Hg is the leading example: 150+ AI staff, 1,600 projects, and about $260M of budgeted profit impact (Hg).

The classic playbook was to install a CFO, an accounting system, a sales system and dashboards. AI makes each of these cheaper (Rillet, an AI accounting system, raised at a $1B valuation). When professionalizing is cheap, sellers can do it before the sale and keep the gain in the price. The advantage moves from installing systems to changing how work gets done.

The spread between good and bad operators is already large. The 90th percentile plant in a US industry is about 1.9 times as productive as the 10th (Syverson). The return gap between top and bottom quartile 2021 funds is 14 points, the widest in a decade. AI is likely to widen both.

Private credit (Q10)

Private credit is $1.5T to $2T and heading toward $4T by 2030 by Moody's count. It financed 214 buyouts for $81B in 2025 (PitchBook). It gives sponsors speed and certainty, at a price about 1.6 points above syndicated loans.

Stress is visible in 2026. 11% of loans pay some interest in more debt instead of cash, and the rate of "bad" versions of that is 6.2%. Lenders took over $22.3B of companies in the first half of 2026, nearly the 2025 full-year total. Investors in the large non-traded credit funds asked for $15B back in Q1 2026 and received 53% (PitchBook). Software is 20 to 35% of these loan books.

Exits, cash back, and funds that cannot raise (Q11, Q12, Q13)

  • The backlog is 32,000 companies worth $3.8T, up from 29,000 a year earlier. 40% have been held more than five years (Bain 2026).

  • Cash returned to investors was 14% of fund value in 2025, below 15% for four straight years.

  • Sales of fund stakes and continuation funds (a firm selling a company to a new fund it also manages) hit $226B to $240B in 2025. Continuation funds are about 14% of exits. They help and do not clear the backlog.

  • Buyout fundraising fell 16% in 2025 and the number of funds closed fell 23%. In the US, only 23 first-time funds closed in the first half of 2026 against 181 a year in 2021 to 2023. A record 304 firms went inactive in 2025.

  • Investors now weigh cash returned as heavily as paper gains. That shortens holds, favors smaller and more saleable companies, and pushes firms toward dividend loans and continuation funds.

  • Retail money was meant to fill the gap: $204B went into semi-liquid PE vehicles in 2025, and an August 2025 executive order opens retirement plans. The 2026 redemption wave shows that money leaves quickly.

What becomes available: experienced deal teams without a fund (independent sponsors have doubled in five years), orphaned portfolios, and old assets priced below stated value.

AI as a threat to existing portfolios (Q14)

Software was the largest PE sector for a decade. In early February 2026, after Anthropic released workplace plug-ins, software and data stocks lost about $285B in days: Thomson Reuters fell 16%, RELX 14% (coverage). US PE software deal value was $16.2B from January to May 2026 against $156B in all of 2025. Lincoln marks weaker software loans at 87% of face value. Most exposed: software priced per user for clerical work, outsourced call centers, IT staffing, marketing agencies and education content.

Buy-and-build, labor and demographics (Q15, Q17, Q18)

Roll-ups work where thousands of small firms have aging owners, low technology use, local markets, and a price gap between small and large. Today that means heating and plumbing, fire and safety inspection, accounting, IT service providers, insurance brokerage, wealth advisors, testing and engineering firms. Roll-ups that relied only on the price gap returned 1.4 times invested capital, against 2.2 times when they also grew profit.

Where it has broken: physician groups (a 2025 FTC settlement with Welsh Carson, and Oregon's 2025 law limiting corporate control of practices), debt-funded auto parts (First Brands), and any sector where technicians leave after the sale. Labor is the limit: 88% of contractors say craft jobs are as hard or harder to fill, and 29% were affected by immigration enforcement in the past six months (AGC, Sept 2026).

Demographics favor services that cannot be offshored, recur every year and serve older customers: home and building services, elder care suppliers, accounting, wealth management, funeral and estate services. Succession supplies the targets, and labor scarcity gives pricing power. The same scarcity breaks roll-ups that do not retain staff.

Where an entrant could sit

  1. An AI deployment arm for portfolio companies, paid partly on profit gained and reused across a sponsor's portfolio. The buyer has money, urgency and no internal staff.

  2. A permanent holding company in a fragmented service sector. It avoids the exit problem that the fund model cannot solve.

  3. A home for stranded teams and orphaned assets from funds that cannot raise.

  4. Retention and training systems for technician-heavy roll-ups, where losing staff is what kills the deal.

What is overstated. That operations drive PE returns. That the mid-market is less competitive. That AI will lift portfolio profit quickly. That private credit is safer than banks. That retail money is stable.

3. The agentic internet and small-business digital infrastructure

Thesis. AI assistants are changing how customers find businesses faster than how they buy from them. The change that matters for a small business today is that searches end without a click, not that robots check out on its behalf. The open protocols are already free and owned by neutral foundations, so they are not a business. Every working implementation runs through a platform the merchant already uses (Google, Shopify, Square, Yelp). The gaps are for businesses off those platforms, for service businesses whose price and availability live in someone's head, and for the trust and liability layer that nobody has built.

How big is the shift (Q7, Q15)

  • 68% of US Google searches ended without a click in early 2026, up from 60% in 2024. AI summaries show on more than 20% of queries and cut clicks by about 60% (SparkToro).

  • Google search revenue still grew 17% to $63.3B in Q2 2026 (Alphabet). Queries did not fall. Clicks out did.

  • ChatGPT sent 0.53% of search referral traffic in August 2026 against Google's 88.55%, by Cloudflare's measure.

  • AI-referred visits to US retail sites grew 693% over the 2025 holidays, then 393%, 269% and 138% in later readings. Those visitors now convert 42% better than others (Adobe).

  • Shopify says AI-driven orders roughly tripled year over year in Q2 2026, and its CEO calls AI a complement to search.

  • Only 10% of AI-assisted shoppers finish the purchase inside the AI tool. 69% go to the retailer's site (eMarketer). 14% of consumers trust AI to buy on its own.

  • Forecasts for 2030 run from $190B (Morgan Stanley, US) to $1T (McKinsey, US). The spread comes from whether "influenced by" or "executed by" an agent is counted. The 2026 figure is about $20B, 1.5% of US e-commerce.

For paid acquisition, the result so far is more ad spending. Organic clicks shrink, so small businesses pay for placement on Google, and now inside ChatGPT. Perplexity dropped ads altogether. Amazon blocks outside agents to protect its $70B-plus sponsored listings business. Advertising survives. Free organic traffic is what goes away.

What decides whether an assistant recommends a business (Q2, Q6, Q8, Q14)

For brands, the strongest predictor of showing up in AI answers is how often the brand is mentioned across the web (correlation 0.66). Backlinks, the classic search signal, score 0.22 (Ahrefs). Reputation elsewhere beats edits to your own site.

For local businesses, a study of 200,085 searches found the business's own website supplied 42% of citations, Google Business Profile 28.6% and Yelp 9.5%. Yelp appears in about 80% of ChatGPT's local answers (BrightLocal, Sept 2026).

The tools sold to measure this are weak. The same prompt returns the same list of brands less than 1 time in 100, so any "rank in AI" number is noise (SparkToro). 28% of sites publish an llms.txt file for AI crawlers and 97% of those files were never requested (Ahrefs). Google says no special files or markup are needed beyond normal search practice and current merchant and business profiles (Google). Money has still poured in: Profound raised at a $1B valuation in February 2026 and Adobe is buying Semrush for $1.9B. Measurement will be bundled. One system can serve people, search engines and agents, and that system is accurate structured data plus a real reputation.

No business controls what an assistant says about it. There is no correction process with a deadline at any AI company. The only lever is fixing the sources. 97% of people who use AI for local recommendations still check reviews.

Checkout, trust and the back office (Q10, Q12, Q17, Q18)

Payments infrastructure is ahead of demand. Visa counted "hundreds" of agent transactions by December 2025, and Mastercard's first live one was in March 2026. Google's payment mandate standard is still version 0.2 a year after launch. The open problems are consent, fraud and disputes. They are rule-making problems for card networks more than software problems.

Trust is thin. The FTC's fake review rule has been in force since October 2024. Its first warning letters went to 10 local firms in December 2025, and no penalties were reported by mid-2026. Agents read whole review histories at scale, so fake reviews matter more. Signals based on outcomes (returns, repeat purchase, license status, complaints) are under-built.

Inside the business, software vendors are opening their systems to agents, but permissions are coarse. The main safety control is read-only mode. There is no common approval or audit layer across accounting, point of sale and customer systems. Incumbents are absorbing agents: Shopify's assistant grew daily active merchants 3.6 times and handled 34M conversations in a quarter. Per-user pricing for clerical software is under pressure, as the February 2026 software selloff showed.

Proprietary knowledge (Q16)

A business should publish what helps it get chosen: catalog, price bands, availability, policies, credentials. It should keep private what lets it win: cost and margin logic, customer histories, supplier terms, how it quotes hard jobs. The interface between the two is an agent that can answer a specific question ("can you do this job Thursday for under $900?") without exposing the rule behind the answer. That interface does not exist for small businesses today.

Who does the work (Q19)

History says small businesses adopt late and through defaults. They got a free Google profile, a Facebook page and a website bundled with their payment terminal. Value went to the demand aggregator (Google, Meta) and to systems of record with payments attached (Shopify, Square, Toast). Pure "presence" tools stayed small. Yext, which synced business listings, reached $355M of revenue and then shrank in value once Google's free profile became the source of truth. The tools that track AI visibility today have the same shape.

The same is happening now. Shopify switched its merchants on. Google uses the feeds it already has. 58% of small businesses say they use AI, but the Census measure of AI used in production is near 10% (not verified this session). The work that remains is for businesses off the big platforms, services with quote-based pricing, reputation building, and answering the phone. Those are service-heavy jobs that fit vertical software vendors and AI-native agencies.

Where an entrant could sit

  2. Permission and audit controls across small-business systems once agents hold the keys to accounting, payments and customer data.

  4. Dispute and liability tooling for agent-placed orders.

Each one competes with a platform default. The diligence question for all five is whether the platforms will distribute it or ship it themselves.

What is overstated. Checkout moving into chatbots. AI replacing Google. AI traffic being large today. AI optimization as a new discipline needing new tools. Open protocols leveling the field for small merchants. Trillion-dollar 2030 forecasts.

Implications for the other sections

  1. Small-business infrastructure (section 3). The connector is the missing "way for agents to act" in the asset table. This raises the value of opportunity 1 in that section and changes its form. The goal is no longer to sell availability software to each business. It is to become the home services or auto repair connector on every assistant.

  2. Succession and the holding company thesis (section 1). One connector across a portfolio of local service firms, with live availability and pricing, is a distribution advantage that a lone owner cannot match. This widens the gap between platform-owned and independent firms. It also lowers the sale value of firms that stay invisible to agents.

  3. Mid-market PE (section 2). "Agent strategy" joins the playbook a portfolio AI team would deploy.   4. Enterprise compute and the privacy line (section 4). This is the consumer version of Latham's worry. Connecting hands the platform your demand, conversion and pricing data. 4. Non-tech companies buying their own GPUs

Thesis. Both the bull case and the skeptic case are partly right. Owned compute is a durable choice for a small set of industries where the data is unique and the workload is steady: trading firms, drug makers, defense. For law firms, banks and most others it is a modest hedge against vendor pricing and a statement to clients. The spend is small for them, the work is fine-tuning (adjusting an existing open model), and the model itself is not the asset. The asset is the cleaned data, the rights to use it, and the tests that show a model works. The missing layer is an accountable operator for firms too small to staff this themselves.

The anchor case, verified

The Financial Times story ran about September 10, 2026. I could not open the FT page, so details come from other coverage.

  • Latham bought Nvidia H200 servers over about three years and keeps them in a third-party data center. It is fine-tuning Nvidia's open Nemotron models. It has about 900 technology staff, about 100 on AI, and $8.3B of revenue. It still uses Harvey firm-wide (Bloomberg Law, Legal IT Insider).

  • Server count and spend are undisclosed. One outlet says "tens of millions annually"; nobody else confirms it.

  • CIO Rene Mendoza gives two reasons: some client information is too sensitive for any cloud vendor, and "a lot of consumption costs coming". Part of the cost worry is Legora's move to usage-based pricing (Non-Billable).

  • The skeptic view: token costs do not change the game for a firm this size, truly secret data need not go into any model, and the point is to make clients feel the firm is doing something (Artificial Lawyer).

  • Latham is not alone. Kirkland & Ellis committed $500M over 3 to 4 years with Palantir, has 180+ engineers and data scientists on it, and is hiring for on-premise GPU clusters (Bloomberg Law).

Vendor cost studies overstate the case by comparing with list prices at the largest clouds. The real answer depends on utilization, and a law firm's fine-tuning jobs come in bursts. But 2026 showed the other side: newest-generation capacity sold out for months, memory prices rose 4 to 5 times, and small server makers had 35 to 40% of their orders filled against 70% for the largest buyers (Tom's Hardware). Owners were hedged. Small buyers cannot get rack-scale systems, which need liquid cooling and over 120 kW per rack. They can get air-cooled servers in ordinary colocation space, which is what Latham did.

Financing fits partnerships badly. Partners are paid out each year, so capital spending comes from current partners' pay and benefits future ones. That pushes firms toward leasing and colocation. The 2025 tax law's full first-year write-off for equipment works in the other direction for a pass-through firm (reported; not verified on a primary page). Large GPU lenders ignore $1M to $20M deals, and equipment makers' finance arms dominate them.

Does a private model beat a frontier model with your documents? (Q4, Q5, Q14)

Mostly no, with one exception.

  • BloombergGPT, trained from scratch on financial data in 2023, was matched by general models within months.

  • Harvey began with a custom model, moved to using several frontier models, and in 2026 started tuning open models on individual firms' workflows. It says results "approach frontier performance" and it falls back to frontier models (Artificial Lawyer).

  • In the one independent legal benchmark, every winning tool ran on frontier models (Vals).

  • Enterprise use of open models fell from 19% to 11% of workloads in 2025, and 76% of AI solutions were bought, not built (Menlo). Buyers told a16z that fine-tuning is declining because long context windows replace it.

  • The exception: narrow tasks with a checkable answer, where a small tuned model matches a frontier model at far lower cost. Open models trail closed ones by about three months (Epoch).

Every new base model forces a re-tune, roughly every 3 to 6 months. The lasting asset is the data pipeline and the test suite.

A law firm's data is also less usable than it looks. Clients own their files. A model tuned across many matters cannot enforce the information barriers firms must keep between clients, because models can leak what they were trained on. The safer design keeps documents in a searchable store with per-matter access controls and tunes only on style and workflow.

Supply of strong open models is a risk. Meta's flagship went closed in 2026. The best open models are Chinese (Qwen, DeepSeek), and many regulated buyers will not run them; US defense agencies are barred from DeepSeek. Nvidia is now the main US supplier of open models because it sells the hardware. If open releases slow, owned servers are left running older models.

The value chain and the missing layer (Q6, Q8, Q11, Q15)

Margins are high at the chip and thin at the server. The software is largely free. What a non-tech buyer lacks is one party that takes responsibility for the whole life of the system: re-tuning on each new base model, testing, access controls, financing and hosting. Latham filled that with 100 staff. Kirkland rents 35 of its 50 AI engineers from partners.

Labor is the binding constraint below the top 20 firms. AI labs pay over $1M and banks pay $300K to $600K (pay figures from memory, not verified). Law firms cannot give equity to non-lawyers under ethics rules.

Vendors are closing the gap from their side. Google ships Gemini on customer premises. Cohere and Mistral deploy privately. Thomson Reuters offers instances behind a firm's firewall. Harvey tunes per-firm models. If the top 10 firms build in house, Harvey and Legora lose prestige accounts and keep most of the market, and their answer is to become the tuning and operations layer.

Is the model an asset the firm can own? (Q17)

In principle, yes. A partnership has no enterprise value today because its know-how leaves with its partners. A data pipeline and tuned model is the first asset that stays. A management company could own it and the hardware and take outside capital. McDermott began assessing PE investment through such a structure in November 2025. In practice, clients can object to training on their files, departing partners will claim their know-how, and mergers create conflicts. Nobody can yet prove that a model has forgotten something.

Where value is trapped, and where an entrant could sit (Q20)

The chain runs from client files, to cleaning and rights clearance, to a searchable store with access controls, to tests, to the model, to workflow, to the bill. Value is trapped at four points. Rights and consent are not productized by anyone. Cleaning is slow because document systems hold messy data. No independent tests exist for firm-specific work. Owned GPUs sit idle between tuning runs.

  1. Managed private AI for regulated mid-size firms. Single-tenant, attested, with model updates handled. Contested: Thomson Reuters, Harvey, Cohere, Mistral and Noxtua are converging on it.

  2. Evaluation, audit and information-barrier assurance, including testing what a model has memorized. Open.

  3. Data preparation and consent management inside document systems. Open.

  4. Consortium compute for firms ranked 50 to 200. Solves idle hardware and talent. Conflicts and trust are the obstacles.

  5. Re-tuning as a service: move a firm's tuning to each new base model automatically.

  6. Small-ticket GPU financing with a guaranteed resale value, bundled with operations.

Verdict on durable versus signaling (synthesis Q16). Durable for trading, pharma, defense and data publishers. For law and most professional services it is a hedge that stays small, and it reverses for most firms once vendors deploy privately at a fair price. The consumption-pricing fear is the part most likely to persist.

5. Synthesis

The central finding. The best opportunities are operating companies, or deployment teams paid on results, that change how work gets done inside ordinary firms. Selling software to small, fragmented, low-budget buyers is the weakest position in every section. The strongest single convergence is aging owners, labor scarcity, the PE price ladder and AI back-office automation meeting in service firms that earn $0.5M to $5M.

How the trends connect

Read it this way: succession supplies the firms, labor scarcity gives them pricing power, and AI supplies cheaper coordination. Every AI branch ends in the same place, a wider gap between firms that can deploy it and firms that cannot. The winner either closes that gap for others or owns the firms and closes it inside them.

16Structural or temporary?Structural: owner age, zero-click search, assistants as the front door, higher cost of debt for PE. Cyclical or likely to reverse: the GPU shortage, the exit drought in its current severity, retail inflows to private credit, in-chat checkout hype, owned GPUs at professional-services firms
17Which are traps?AI visibility dashboards, llms.txt tools, software for tiny firms, physician roll-ups, roll-ups that rely only on the price gap, thin connectors with no unique supply, connector agencies as a long-term business
18High willingness to pay, poorly served?Owners of $1M to $5M firms who do not know what they are worth. PE sponsors who need portfolio AI results. Mid-size regulated firms that want private AI
19Where can AI create an operating company?Accounting and tax, IT services, small-business brokerage, insurance agency, claims and billing. Thrive, General Catalyst and Teamshares are early proof. Results are still sponsor-reported
20When is owning data, a model or compute a real advantage?When data is unique, clearly owned and the task has a checkable answer: trading, drug discovery, legal publishing. It is a distraction when the data belongs to clients, the load is bursty, and frontier models with your documents already win
21Does AI capability widen the gap between large and small firms?Yes, in the near term. Large firms can staff deployment and small ones cannot, and 76% of enterprise AI is bought. That raises the value of joining a platform, which speeds consolidation and helps buy-and-build. It also lowers the value of unimproved small firms at sale. Cheap vendor tools narrow the gap later
22Should a PE sponsor own shared compute and model capability?Shared deployment teams, data plumbing, test suites and vendor contracts: yes, and Hg already does this. Shared GPUs: no for most. Portfolio companies' data cannot be pooled across owners, loads are small, and rented capacity plus frontier models is cheaper
23Can a company model preserve a retiring owner's knowledge?This is the most interesting open link. Owner dependence is the top reason small firms do not sell, and a jump from $1M to $5M of profit doubles the multiple. A captured record of how the owner prices, quotes and handles customers would raise transferability. It needs a searchable record and workflows, not a trained model or owned GPUs. I found no product doing it
24Where is the line between publishing to agents and keeping data private?Publish what gets you chosen: catalog, price bands, availability, policies, credentials. Keep what lets you win: costs, margin rules, customer histories, client files. The interface is an agent that answers a specific question without revealing the rule. The same logic applies to a law firm: market the capability, keep the matter data behind access controls
25Strongest overall combination?See the scoring table. The top two both involve carrying out AI-led change inside ordinary service firms, as owner or as hired operator

What would change these conclusions

  • Audited results from the AI roll-ups. If Thrive or General Catalyst companies show margin gains in filed numbers, the holdco thesis strengthens a lot. If not, it is ordinary PE with a story.

  • Sponsor surveys turning. If the 39% of sponsors who expect no AI impact falls sharply, portfolio deployment becomes a crowded market quickly.

  • Meta's connector fee and ranking rule, and card networks publishing liability rules for agent purchases. These decide who captures value in agent commerce.

  • Open model releases slowing or stopping would end the owned-compute trend outside trading and pharma.
